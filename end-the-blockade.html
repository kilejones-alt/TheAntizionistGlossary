END THE BLOCKADE

“End the blockade” is a demand to lift Israel’s land and naval restrictions on Gaza. At its most specific, it can mean wider humanitarian access, more predictable crossings, and less damage to civilian life. Antizionists use it to mean the removal of Israeli control over Gaza’s sea access, border inspections, dual-use restrictions, and cargo screening, with little said about Hamas’s arms supply or Egypt’s role at Rafah. The narrow version is a humanitarian request. The broad version is a security demand wearing humanitarian clothes.

Israel withdrew its soldiers and settlements from Gaza in 2005. Human Rights Watch reported that Palestinian armed groups fired about 2,700 Qassam-type rockets into Israel between September 2005 and May 2007, and that those attacks violated the laws of war because the rockets were too inaccurate to distinguish military targets from civilians. Hamas seized the Strip in June 2007. Israel tightened its land-crossing restrictions after that takeover, while Egypt controlled the southern exit through Rafah. The ICRC casebook states that Israel established the naval blockade on January 3, 2009, and announced it on January 6. The slogan starts the story after the security problem has been edited out.

Arms-smuggling by sea predated the naval blockade. Israel says the Santorini, intercepted in May 2001, carried a large weapons shipment moving from Lebanon toward Gaza. In January 2002, Israel seized the Karine A and reported a cargo of 50 tons of advanced weapons, among them rockets and rifles, mortar shells, mines and anti-tank weapons. The chant asks the listener to forget both ships.

The San Remo Manual permits naval blockades in armed conflict when the legal conditions are met. It forbids any blockade whose sole purpose is to starve civilians or deny them survival goods, and it bars disproportionate harm to civilians. The UN’s Palmer Panel concluded in 2011 that Israel’s naval blockade was a legitimate security measure to keep weapons from reaching Gaza by sea and complied with international law. It accepted that Israel faced armed attacks from Gaza and that the blockade was adopted to defend Israeli territory and civilians. The same report called Israel’s land-access policy unsustainable and in need of change. A separate UN Human Rights Council fact-finding mission reached the opposite conclusion about the closure and blockade.

“End the blockade” turns up in flotilla campaigns and BDS materials, at campus protests and ceasefire rallies, in NGO statements and across social media. The Free Gaza Movement’s 2008 voyages from Cyprus made it visible as a direct-action demand, and the 2010 flotilla and the Mavi Marmara incident gave it international reach. The flotillas set out to break the blockade at sea, and the UN fact-finding mission itself described the flotilla’s primary aim as public pressure against the blockade.

The slogan lumps four distinct things together. Land crossings govern the movement of people, food, medicine, commercial goods, fuel, and construction materials. The naval blockade concerns sea access and the interdiction of weapons. Rafah is an Egyptian border crossing. Hamas governs Gaza internally and has used tunnels, rockets, and hostage-taking in its war against Israel. All of it is folded into a single demand pointed at one country.

Egypt has restricted movement through Rafah, moved against smuggling tunnels, and demolished buildings on its own side to create a buffer zone. Human Rights Watch reported that Egyptian authorities razed thousands of structures along the Gaza border between 2013 and 2015. The global protest vocabulary almost never says “End the Egyptian blockade.” Israel is treated as the only actor on Gaza’s borders with any agency.

The slogan travels beside “open Gaza,” “break the siege,” and “stop starving Gaza.” It refuses to say what replaces inspection and arms interdiction once the restrictions are gone. Inspections and dual-use lists, naval warnings and cargo routing through Israeli ports all read as punishment, and the question of how to stop Hamas from importing weapons while civilians receive what they need is pushed off the table. Hamas’s choices, Egypt’s border policy, Palestinian factional conflict, rocket fire, tunnels, and weapons shipments are left out, leaving only Israel on stage. Anyone who supports inspection or arms interdiction is called a supporter of hunger. A speaker who means the removal of all Israeli inspection and sea control should name the security consequence that follows.
