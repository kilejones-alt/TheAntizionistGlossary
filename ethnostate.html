ETHNOSTATE

“Ethnostate” is used in antizionist speech to present Israel as a country built for Jews at everyone else’s expense. Its users point to the Law of Return and Jewish national symbols, Hebrew and the 2018 Nation-State Basic Law, and Israel’s unresolved conflict with Palestinians in the West Bank, Gaza, and East Jerusalem. They reduce all of that to the claim that a Jewish state is racist by its very design. In ordinary English after Charlottesville, “ethnostate” calls up white-nationalist dreams of a racial homeland. Applied to Israel, the word drags Jewish self-determination into that field of association. A state founded as a refuge after centuries of expulsions, pogroms and the Holocaust is made to sound like a racial project modeled on exclusion.

Israel has a Jewish national character in the Law of Return, Jewish public holidays, Jewish symbols, Hebrew civic life, and a constitutional commitment to the Jewish people. It is a Jewish-majority state with democratic institutions and formal citizenship rights for Arab citizens, in a long war with enemies who have often rejected a Jewish state anywhere in the land. Sammy Smooha described Israel as an ethnic democracy, Yoav Peled examined Israeli citizenship through liberal, republican, and ethno-national strands, and Oren Yiftachel used “ethnocracy” to analyze land, borders, and unequal access to power. “Ethnostate” is no recognized category in Freedom House, V-Dem, the Economist Intelligence Unit, or Polity.

Use of the term grew in anti-Israel commentary after the Nation-State Basic Law passed in July 2018. That law declares Israel the nation-state of the Jewish people, names Hebrew the state language, calls Jewish settlement a national value, and states that national self-determination in Israel is unique to the Jewish people. Critics read it as proof that Israel had placed Jewish nationality above civic equality. The law cancelled no individual right, and Arab citizens kept their votes and their citizenship. Israel has no single written constitution, so its Basic Laws, ordinary statutes, Supreme Court rulings, and democratic practice are read together. The term spread further after the November 2022 election, when Netanyahu’s coalition took in hard-right religious-nationalist parties, and during the 2023 court crisis. After October 7 and the Gaza war, it appeared beside apartheid, settler-colonialism, genocide, and Jewish supremacy, in campus protests, faculty letters, NGO commentary, and online campaigns.

The term treats Israel’s Jewish features as instruments of ethnic domination. The Law of Return is called racial exclusion, Hebrew and Jewish symbols supremacy, and the Nation-State Law proof that non-Jews are permanent outsiders. Checkpoints in the West Bank, border controls around Gaza, settlement growth, and the disadvantages facing Arab citizens are offered as evidence of one seamless Jewish order. That reading merges Arab citizens of Israel with West Bank Palestinians, Gaza Palestinians with East Jerusalem residents, and Israeli civil law with Palestinian Authority administration, Hamas rule, military control and refugee claims.

Arab citizens of Israel vote and hold Knesset seats, run parties and serve as judges, study in universities, work in hospitals and litigate in court. In 2021, Ra’am joined the Bennett-Lapid coalition, which the Israel Democracy Institute describes as the first time an Arab party was a formal member of an Israeli government. In 2022, Khaled Kabub, an Arab Muslim judge, joined Israel’s Supreme Court. Israel’s April 2026 Independence Day statistics put the population at 10.244 million, including 2.157 million Arabs, or 21.1 percent. West Bank Palestinians lack Israeli citizenship because the area has not been folded into Israel’s ordinary civil order, and Gaza Palestinians have lived under Hamas since 2007. Freedom House’s 2026 report rates Israel Free at 73 out of 100 and assesses the West Bank and Gaza separately.

The Law of Return gives Jews a path to immigration based on Jewish connection. Diaspora-return laws are common, and many states grant preferred citizenship to members of a historic people or national kin group. Israel’s law answers Jewish history, which is exile and statelessness, genocide, and the absence of any other Jewish refuge.

The Oxford English Dictionary traces the earliest known use of “ethnostate” to a 1985 glossary by Fred W. Riggs, where it was merely descriptive. It darkened through white-nationalist writing. Wilmot Robertson’s The Ethnostate, composed between 1985 and 1992, used the term for a white racial homeland, and later figures including Richard Spencer made it public shorthand for racial separatism. By the time it reached mass debate after the 2017 Charlottesville rally, it sounded supremacist. Scholars of Israel and Palestine, among them Smooha, Peled, Yiftachel, Rouhana, Ghanem and Hirschl, had long debated Jewish statehood and ethnic democracy, ethnocracy, citizenship and minority rights. Antizionists took the harsher public word and pinned it to Israel. Beneath that sits an older polemic, the Soviet and Arab nationalist writing that treated Zionism as racism or colonialism, given international status by UN General Assembly Resolution 3379 in 1975. The resolution was revoked in 1991, and its logic outlived it.

The term makes Jewish statehood sound disqualifying. Once Israel is an ethnostate, the argument drifts from specific law and policy to the country’s basic character, and boycott and divestment, academic and cultural exclusion, arms embargoes and diplomatic isolation all sound like anti-racist duties. A flag bearing a Jewish symbol, an immigration law, Jewish holidays, Hebrew, and state concern for Jews abroad are suspect in a way the same features rarely are elsewhere. That selective treatment is the double standard flagged by Natan Sharansky’s 3D test and by several definitions of antisemitism. The many countries that have established religions, majority national identities, ancestry-based citizenship, or high ethnic homogeneity are rarely branded with the same word. The Jewish state grew from Jewish peoplehood and ancestral connection, modern nationalism, persecution, and the collapse of Jewish safety across Europe and the Middle East.
