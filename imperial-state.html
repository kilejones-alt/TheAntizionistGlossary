IMPERIAL STATE

“Imperial State” is an antizionist label that casts Israel as a regional hegemon reaching for dominion beyond its recognized borders through force and settlement, intelligence operations, and its alliance with the United States. It borrows the vocabulary of empire and pins it on a country of roughly ten million people living in a hostile security environment. In this use the Jewish state itself is an imperial entity whose power is illegitimate by nature and whose survival is said to depend on domination.

The label is often welded to the “Greater Israel” story, the claim that Israel secretly covets territory from the Nile to the Euphrates. Maximalist currents exist on the Israeli far right, and annexation and settlement in the West Bank are disputed. The Nile-to-Euphrates version is not the border policy of the State of Israel, and antizionist usage converts fringe maximalists into proof of a state-wide secret plan.

Nur Masalha’s Imperial Israel and the Palestinians: The Politics of Expansion (2000) gave the term a scholarly shape by arguing that Israel’s post-1967 conduct toward Palestinians revealed expansionist tendencies, and Maxime Rodinson’s earlier Israel: A Colonial-Settler State? (1973) set Zionism inside a broader history of European settlement. In current usage “imperial Israel” folds targeted killings and strikes against Hezbollah, operations in Gaza and settlement growth, normalization agreements, intelligence reach, U.S. aid and border enforcement into one project. In November 2025, New York Times reporting by Roger Cohen and David Guttenfelder ran under the headline “‘Imperial Israel’ in the New Middle East.”

The term carries a contradiction common in anti-Israel rhetoric. Israel is described as an American outpost and, at the same time, as the hidden hand directing American policy. It lets Israel appear dependent and omnipotent together, small and world-shaping.

To call Israel imperial is to read every Israeli use of power as domination. Security zones are colonies, deterrence is expansion, strikes on armed groups are regional rule, and peace treaties are tactical pauses. Israel returned the entire Sinai Peninsula to Egypt under the 1979 peace treaty and completed the withdrawal in 1982, giving up territory larger than itself to make peace. It withdrew from southern Lebanon in 2000, a withdrawal the UN Secretary-General reported as completed in line with Security Council Resolution 425. It dismantled every Israeli settlement in Gaza during the 2005 disengagement. The term makes Israeli power illegitimate by category while treating the power of Hamas, Hezbollah, Iran, or Syria as mere reaction.

The use of “imperial” against Zionism begins with the Bolsheviks. Lenin dismissed Zionism as “bourgeois nationalism” and a reactionary movement that, in his view, deceived Jewish workers and held them back from assimilation and class struggle. Stalin’s 1913 pamphlet Marxism and the National Question argued that the Jews failed the test of nationhood, lacking a common territory and a shared economic life, and were better understood as a caste than a people. That theory later licensed Soviet treatment of Jewish particularism as disloyalty. After Israel existed, the editorial apparatus attached to Stalin’s collected works recast Zionist organizations as “the agents of the American imperialists” working against the USSR and the revolutionary movement. An old objection to Jewish nationalism now presented Zionism as the servant, and then the partner, of empire.

From that root the idea grew along two branches. The first was Soviet propaganda after the Six-Day War, which portrayed Zionism as an arm of Western imperialism. Yuri Ivanov’s Caution: Zionism!, published in English by Progress Publishers in 1970, tied Zionism to imperialist power, finance, and propaganda. General Assembly Resolution 3151 G grouped “Zionism and Israeli imperialism” with South African racism and Portuguese colonialism in 1973, and Resolution 3379 declared Zionism a form of racism in 1975. The second branch developed in Arab nationalist and Palestinian liberation literature. It cast Zionism as a European implant, Israel as a forward base of Western power, and the Palestinian struggle as part of a wider anti-colonial fight. Rodinson’s 1967 essay supplied a Marxist version of this analysis, and Masalha’s book later applied it to settlement policy.

As a status label, “Imperial State” sets Israel beside Britain in India or France in Algeria. The question it poses is why an empire should be tolerated at all. Hamas’s October 7 massacre, Hezbollah’s arsenal and cross-border fire, Iranian sponsorship, Houthi attacks, Syrian instability, hostage-taking, and the earlier Arab-state wars are passed over in favor of Israel’s supposed drive for rule. Israel is a small state with unusual military reach because it faces nearby enemies, narrow borders, and a dense regional threat network. Sinai, southern Lebanon, and Gaza do not fit a story in which expansion is the unchanging aim.
