FREE PALESTINE

“Free Palestine” leaves its object blank. It can mean Palestinian self-determination, civil rights, or a negotiated state alongside Israel. If Palestine is to be freed from military rule in the West Bank, Hamas rule in Gaza, Palestinian Authority repression, corruption, poverty, or statelessness, the slogan points toward ordinary civic claims. If it is to be freed from Israel’s existence as a Jewish state, the slogan denies Jewish self-determination.

The Palestine Liberation Organization was founded in 1964, before Israel controlled the West Bank or Gaza. Its 1968 Palestinian National Charter is central to this history. Article 5 defined Palestinians as Arab nationals who normally resided in Palestine until 1947. Article 6 counted as Jewish nationals only those who had normally resided there before what it termed the Zionist invasion. Article 9 made armed struggle the path to liberation, and Article 19 declared partition and Israel’s establishment illegal. Article 20 denied Jewish nationhood, and Article 21 rejected any substitute for total liberation. The modern slogan inherits a vocabulary in which liberation meant replacing Israel.

Arab nationalist leaders in the 1950s and 1960s cast Palestine as a cause of regional redemption, and the PLO gave that cause an organizational form. After 1967 the phrase moved through Third World revolutionary networks, Soviet-aligned antizionist propaganda, student movements, and Palestinian solidarity campaigns, attached to anti-imperial and anti-colonial idioms that made the conflict legible to Western activists. By the 2001 Durban NGO Forum and the 2005 BDS call, Palestinian liberation language had been repackaged in the terms of apartheid, colonialism, occupation, return, equality, and boycott. Modern campus usage inherits the rights-based appeal and the older eliminationist demand together.

Since October 7, 2023, “Free Palestine” has turned up across campus encampments and street protests, social media and graffiti, clothing, concert stages and institutional statements. The ADL describes the phrase as widely used by people advocating Palestinian rights and states that, since October 7, it has been used in numerous cases to intimidate or attack Jews and Jewish institutions.

In the May 21, 2025 attack outside the Capital Jewish Museum in Washington, D.C., Yaron Lischinsky and Sarah Milgrim, both Israeli Embassy employees, were killed after leaving an American Jewish Committee event. According to the February 2026 superseding filing summarized by the Justice Department, Elias Rodriguez allegedly fired about twenty shots and called out “Free Palestine.” The Justice Department also stated that he displayed a red keffiyeh inside the museum and said, “I did it for Palestine. I did it for Gaza.” On June 1, 2025, a second attack targeted a Run for Their Lives gathering in Boulder, Colorado, held to draw attention to the Israeli hostages in Gaza. The Justice Department says Mohamed Sabry Soliman approached the group with Molotov cocktails and shouted “Free Palestine” while throwing one of them. The federal case alleged hate-crime counts and described a handwritten document naming Zionism an enemy until Jerusalem is liberated and Jews are expelled from the land. Jews hear the phrase with those attacks in mind.

The chant sets aside Hamas’s rule in Gaza since 2007 and the Palestinian Authority’s authoritarian rule in the West Bank. It minimizes rejected peace proposals and regional manipulation of the Palestinian cause. Egypt controls the Rafah crossing and imposes its own restrictions, and liberation campaigns rarely make Egypt an object of protest.

The blank also lets users skip what comes after liberation, whether two states, a confederation, a single Arab-majority state in place of Israel, full refugee return into Israel, or the end of Zionism altogether. Antizionist movements gain from that looseness, because the phrase travels without forcing anyone to say whether Jewish national survival is part of the future they want. Einat Wilf and Adi Schwartz argue in The War of Return that the maximalist return demand has worked less as a refugee solution than as a means of undoing Israel through demographics. The IHRA working definition identifies the denial of Jewish self-determination, including the claim that Israel’s existence is a racist endeavor, as an example that may be antisemitic depending on context.

In antizionist spaces people are rarely asked to define the phrase. They are asked to say it, and the saying marks who belongs in the moral community and who has to explain himself. A person who wants a Palestinian state beside Israel and a person who wants a Palestine in place of Israel chant the very same words, and the maximalist claim borrows the respectability of human-rights language. The slogan frees Palestine from everyone except its own rulers. It almost never confronts Hamas, the Palestinian Authority, or the Arab states that have used Palestinians as instruments, and it leaves Palestinians under leaders it does not challenge. It is defended as a humane call for rights in one room and shouted at a Jewish institution in another.
