HASBARA

Hasbara is a Hebrew word usually rendered as explanation or public diplomacy. In Israeli use it covers the efforts of the state and civil-society groups, of diplomats, soldiers, students and ordinary citizens, to explain Israel’s position to outside audiences. Every state explains itself. In antizionist usage the word is a dismissal, deployed to brand any pro-Israel correction, any Jewish testimony, any Israeli source or historical fact as propaganda before its content has been examined.

A student who cites Hamas’s charter, a journalist who notes rocket fire from civilian areas, and a Jew who describes fear after October 7 all receive the same one-word answer. It ends the discussion without anyone having to disprove a thing. It treats Israeli speech as uniquely deceptive and Jewish speech about Israel as presumptively fake, instructing the listener to distrust the speaker first and the evidence second.

The term turns up in social-media replies and protest literature, in campus debate and commentary about Israel, usually as an epithet such as cheap hasbara, Zionist hasbara, hasbara troll, hasbara bot, or paid hasbarist. These phrases mean that the source is Israeli or pro-Israel and can be ignored. After October 7, documentation of Hamas atrocities, hostage abuse, rocket fire, tunnel systems, and sexual violence was routinely waved away online as hasbara. The United Nations mission led by Pramila Patten reported in March 2024 that there were reasonable grounds to believe conflict-related sexual violence occurred during the October 7 attacks, including rape and gang rape in at least several locations, and clear and convincing information that hostages had been subjected to sexual violence. Israel’s Foreign Ministry then directed its embassies to promote the report. Critics used the publicity campaign as a reason to dismiss the UN findings.

Matti Friedman, a former Associated Press correspondent, has written about the belief underneath the slur, that Israeli explanations are inherently less credible than the claims of Israel’s enemies. In his 2014 Tablet essay he described how major outlets over-cover Israel, under-cover Palestinian agency, and frequently omit facts that complicate the standard account. A public culture that assumes Israel is always lying has decided the case before any evidence arrives.

Used as a slur, hasbara presumes that Jews and Israelis never speak from conviction or evidence, from memory or lived experience. They speak, supposedly, because they have been trained, bought, manipulated, or mobilized by a state. Aimed at diaspora Jews, it recasts a Jewish student’s grief, a rabbi’s public statement, a hostage family’s testimony, or a scholar’s correction as foreign messaging. The speaker’s Jewishness or Zionism or connection to Israel is the reason not to listen.

The suspicion is old. In earlier antisemitic language, Jews were said to act through hidden coordination, dual loyalty, financial influence, or secret control. The hasbara slur updates the pattern for the internet. The belief that Jews control the press is replaced by the assertion that Jews are performing hasbara, and the notion that Jews take orders from a foreign power by the label of state propagandist. The term often travels with talk of bots, paid trolls, and foreign agents. It erases the line between a government campaign and a Jew with an argument, and Jewish civic participation is treated as a hostile operation.

The word’s Zionist history predates the state. Gideon Kouts traces the formal entry of hasbara into Zionist vocabulary to Nahum Sokolow, and later scholarship describes it as Israel’s public diplomacy, a state and civic effort to explain the country to foreign audiences. Israeli writers have long argued over whether the country has a “hasbara problem,” whether its explaining is too defensive, and whether public diplomacy can repair a policy dispute or only present it more skillfully. The practice is real, with official spokespeople, public-diplomacy units, military communications offices, and diaspora-facing programs.

The hostile use belongs to a separate lineage. Soviet antizionist propaganda after 1967 depicted Zionism as a worldwide apparatus of deception, racism, imperialism, and manipulation. Yuri Ivanov’s Caution: Zionism!, first published in Russian in 1969 and then circulated in translation, was among the foundational texts of that campaign. Izabella Tabarovsky notes that Soviet messaging treated Zionism as a vast hidden force and exported the language through state publishing houses, front groups, and international networks. Today’s hasbara insult inherits that mood, in which Israel deceives and Zionists are agents.

In antizionist settings the word stops a fact from being heard. A document can be public, a source primary, a photograph verified, a quote exact, and the answer can still be hasbara, because the label moves the argument from the truth of the claim to the suspected impurity of the speaker. Challenges to casualty figures, legal claims, maps, slogans, or historical statements can all be dismissed the same way, and weak claims no longer have to survive scrutiny. The epithet isolates Jews in public life. It tells non-Jewish audiences that Jewish testimony about Israel is suspect, tells Jews that their grief and fear and memory will be treated as scripted, and tells institutions that anti-Jewish harassment can be relabeled as opposition to propaganda. The existence of official Israeli public diplomacy does not make ordinary Jews state propagandists.
