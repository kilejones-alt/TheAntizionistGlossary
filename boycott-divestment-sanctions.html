BOYCOTT, DIVESTMENT, SANCTIONS (BDS)

BDS is short for Boycott, Divestment, Sanctions. It is a Palestinian-led campaign launched on July 9, 2005, to isolate Israel through economic and academic, cultural and diplomatic pressure. The official call says Israel should be boycotted, divested from, and sanctioned until it complies with international law and Palestinian rights. Together its demands put the continued existence of a Jewish state in question.

The campaign presents itself as the heir to the South African anti-apartheid boycott. The comparison places Israel inside one of the cleanest twentieth-century stories of pressure and liberation, where boycott was the decent person’s only option. If Israel is South Africa, a boycott is a test of basic conscience, and to decline is to side with the oppressor. The analogy spares the campaign the trouble of proving the comparison.

The BDS call seeks an end to the 1967 occupation and the dismantling of the barrier, full equality for Arab citizens of Israel, and refugee return under UN Resolution 194. The third demand decides everything. Applied literally, it would move several million Palestinian refugees and their descendants into Israel proper, and the Jewish majority that defines the country would vanish. The result is a single binational state and the end of the Jewish one. Omar Barghouti, a co-founder and leading advocate of BDS, rejected the two-state solution in a 2004 article, writing that it was dead and needed an official death certificate. The project aims past the West Bank and Gaza at the existence of the state itself.

On campuses, BDS operates through student-government resolutions and divestment drives, faculty petitions and union votes, speaker disruptions, encampment demands and pressure on university investment committees. It is usually carried by Students for Justice in Palestine and Faculty for Justice in Palestine chapters, by Jewish Voice for Peace, and by allied coalitions. Their message is that universities must not invest in, cooperate with, or normalize Israel. PACBI, the Palestinian Campaign for the Academic and Cultural Boycott of Israel, calls for boycotting Israeli academic institutions. The boycott is institutional in theory and reaches individual scholars in practice, through cancelled talks and rejected partnerships, conference exclusions and pressure on exchange programs.

Corporate campaigns target companies tied to Israel’s military or security, energy or technology sectors. Cultural campaigns press musicians and filmmakers, artists and athletes not to perform in Israel or work with Israeli institutions. Anti-normalization campaigns oppose joint Israeli-Palestinian projects unless those projects first adopt BDS premises. In every case ordinary contact with Israel is recoded as complicity. The campaign asks institutions and individuals to sever routine ties with the world’s only Jewish-majority state.

After October 7 this language grew more visible in encampment demands and divestment resolutions. In February 2024, both the undergraduate and graduate student-government bodies at UCLA passed resolutions calling for divestment from Israel. In July 2025, UCLA agreed to pay more than $6 million to settle claims brought by Jewish students and a faculty member. The Justice Department found that the university had violated Title VI and the Equal Protection Clause by showing deliberate indifference to a hostile environment for Jewish and Israeli students. In May 2026, the department filed a separate suit alleging that UCLA had tolerated an antisemitic educational environment. UCLA has disputed some of the allegations and points to steps it says it has taken against antisemitism.

No state whose conduct is comparable to or worse than Israel’s faces a global boycott built on the same university infrastructure, professional-association pressure, cultural enforcement, and demand for personal purity. China and Russia, Syria and Iran draw criticism, sanctions and protest in various measures. None of them has been turned into a standing test of whether a person may belong inside a progressive institution. For many diaspora Jews BDS arrives as a demand for disavowal. A Jewish student or scholar, artist or professional who keeps a tie to Israel can be required to renounce it before being accepted as a full participant in campus or cultural life. The instruction is to separate oneself from the Jewish state.

Boycott pressure against Jewish national life in the land is older than the state. On December 2, 1945, the Arab League formally adopted a boycott of Jewish goods and services in Mandatory Palestine, treating Jewish products as undesirable because their production might advance Zionist aims. No Jewish state yet existed, and the target was the Jewish national project itself. After 1948 the Arab League boycott grew layers, a primary boycott of trade with Israel, a secondary boycott of companies doing business with Israel, and a tertiary boycott of companies doing business with those companies. Congressional Research Service reporting describes a long-running mechanism whose enforcement has waxed and waned by state and era.

The modern BDS vocabulary took shape at the 2001 Durban NGO Forum, which branded Israel a racist, apartheid state and called for its isolation. PACBI was founded in Ramallah in 2004, and the broader BDS call followed on July 9, 2005, the first anniversary of the International Court of Justice advisory opinion on Israel’s West Bank barrier. The movement married the older Arab boycott to the Durban apartheid vocabulary and carried both into Western civil society, universities, unions, churches, and cultural bodies. The audience changed and the aim did not.

German domestic-intelligence reporting in 2024 treated BDS as a suspected extremist case and noted links to secular Palestinian extremism, and media summaries of that report stated that BDS was supported by organizations including Hamas and Palestinian Islamic Jihad. BDS is a coalition campaign with loose affiliations, supporters, and aligned organizations. Some European security authorities have concluded that parts of the BDS environment overlap with extremist anti-Israel networks.

The campaign works by isolation. It aims to make Israel costly to engage, costly to defend, and in time costly to recognize as a normal state. Economic boycotts hit commerce, academic boycotts hit research life, and cultural boycotts hit ordinary human exchange. Campus divestment drives train students to treat Israel as the one exception among nations. Jews who reject Israel are elevated as proof of anti-racist virtue, while Jews who defend the country’s existence fall under suspicion. Many Jewish students experience BDS as a social rule, to denounce Israel or risk exclusion.

Anti-normalization supplies the discipline, and the people most devoted to coexistence are made the most suspect. A peace program or exchange seminar, a documentary collaboration or a medical-research partnership can be condemned for keeping Israelis inside the circle of legitimate human contact. That logic punishes the very relationships through which compromise would have to come.

As of the most recent legislation trackers, 38 states had some anti-BDS law or executive order in effect. Some govern state contracts, others state investments. Civil-liberties groups argue that many of these laws burden protected boycotts and political advocacy. Supporters counter that the laws address discriminatory commercial conduct against Israel and, in some cases, against Jews. BDS sits at the meeting point of free expression and anti-discrimination law, and it generates litigation wherever it lands.

Many states commit abuses, and many deserve criticism or sanctions or diplomatic pressure. BDS seeks a standing boycott of one country across economic and academic, cultural and civic life, a permanent quarantine. It calls the refugee demand justice, and the literal application of that demand would end the Jewish state. It turns coexistence into complicity and asks Jews to perform disavowal as the price of admission.
