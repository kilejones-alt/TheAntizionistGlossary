CHILD KILLERS

“Child killers” is an antizionist slogan that casts Israel, Israelis, Zionists, or Jews as a group as the deliberate murderers of Palestinian children. It surfaces in chants and placards, in social media posts and NGO materials and the media’s handling of war casualties. The wording travels with photographs of dead or wounded children and casualty figures stripped of their battlefield context.

Children have died in Gaza. The libel lies in the leap from death to intent. “Child killers” does not claim that Israeli strikes caused civilian deaths, or that a particular strike was unlawful, or that a commander skipped a feasible precaution. It asserts that the purpose was child murder, and it supplies no evidence of purpose.

The words sit inside an older story. Karolina Placzynta analyzes modern child-murder claims about Israel as part of the ritual-murder tradition, citing examples that portray Israelis as habitually or even joyfully killing children. Abraham Foxman made a similar point about earlier Gaza-war rhetoric, calling the phrase a sanitized blood-guilt charge. The words place Jews and the Jewish state inside a centuries-old pattern in which Jewish evil is supposedly proven by the murder of the young.

The slogan moves through street protest and institutional channels. In November 2024, the British surgeon Nizam Mamode told the UK House of Commons International Development Committee that he had seen children with single gunshot wounds to the head and concluded they were “clearly deliberately targeted by Israeli snipers.” The Israeli Embassy in London later challenged parts of his testimony, saying some claims rested on hearsay and calling the allegation of IDF fire on children and uninvolved civilians baseless. An allegation of intentional child-killing had traveled through a parliamentary committee before the underlying evidence had been tested.

The Community Security Trust recorded 542 antisemitic incidents in Britain during July and August 2014, and 48 of them used phrases like “child murderers” or “baby killers” against British Jews, synagogues, and other Jewish targets. CST’s separate discourse report ranked the child- and baby-killer theme as the second most common in antisemitic incidents over that period. The language crossed from protest over Israeli military action into direct harassment of Jews who had nothing to do with it.

Since October 7, 2023, the language has filled campus protests, street demonstrations, and social media campaigns. It clusters near “genocide,” “baby killer,” “murdering children,” and “Zionists kill children.” A person branded a supporter of child murder is marked as someone decent people are expected to shun. At Baruch College in 2024, anti-Israel student groups went after Hillel, the main address for Jewish student life on campus. Hillel at Baruch’s own news page described a demonstration in which protesters said the Jewish campus group was murdering children and backing fascism and genocide. The object was a Jewish student organization in New York, held to account for a war thousands of miles away.

The slogan also draws on more respectable-looking sources. The New York Times’ May 2021 feature “They Were Only Children” placed the photographs and names of children killed in that month’s Gaza fighting into a format normally reserved for memorial. The presentation handed readers a child-centered account in which the military setting and the conduct of Palestinian armed groups receded into the background. Antizionist groups borrow that grammar for blunter ends, that Israel murders children, Zionists support it, and Jews who will not denounce Israel are complicit.

The phrase presumes that Israeli operations target children as a matter of policy. Under international humanitarian law, proving that would require evidence that specific attacks were aimed at children as civilians instead of at lawful military objectives, and that the deaths were intended and were no incidental harm weighed under the rules of distinction, proportionality and precaution. The slogan treats intent as established by the presence of dead children alone. It bypasses how many children died, how the figures were counted, whether the dead were fully identified, and whether militants were included in the totals.

The Associated Press analyzed Gaza Health Ministry figures in June 2024 and reported that the ministry belongs to the Hamas-run government, does not separate civilian from combatant deaths, and publishes daily totals higher than its own fully identified underlying records. The AP also found that the share of women and children among fully identified deaths fell over time, from 64 percent in October 2023 to 54 percent by April 30, 2024, with women and children making up 38 percent of newly and fully identified deaths in April alone. Abraham Wyner’s March 2024 statistical critique noted that the totals for an early reporting window rose with a linearity he found unlikely in active urban warfare.

The slogan erases Hamas from the battlefield it helped create. Hamas embeds military assets in dense civilian areas, operates from tunnels and civilian surroundings, and gains when Israeli strikes produce images of civilian suffering. A phrase that names only one party to a two-sided battlefield is a selective account.

The lineage runs through the ritual-murder myth, which falsely held that Jews killed Christian children for ritual use. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum identifies William of Norwich in 1144 as the first medieval European case and notes that blood libels were a major theme of Jewish persecution across the continent. The myth often surfaced around Passover and could trigger pogroms, trials, executions, and expulsions. Hugh of Lincoln produced another English version in 1255, and the 1840 Damascus Affair carried the myth into the Middle East when the disappearance of a Christian monk led to the torture of Jewish leaders and an international crisis. The 1903 Kishinev pogrom was fueled in part by press reports tying Jews to a Christian child’s death. In the 1928 Massena incident in New York, officials questioned a rabbi after a child went missing, and she was found unharmed. The Nazi paper Der Stürmer devoted its May 1934 issue to ritual murder, and in 1983 the Syrian defense minister Mustafa Tlass published The Matzah of Zion, presenting ritual murder as historical fact. Each of these cases attached child murder to Jews as a people. The antizionist version puts Israel in the place of the Jewish community and wartime child casualties in the place of ritual murder. It rests on the same collective guilt, slain innocence, and the notion that Jews or their state carry a special cruelty toward children.

A person who asks for evidence or context or legal specificity is painted as a defender of murdered children. A lawful-war analysis asks what happened in a given strike, the target, the intelligence, the warning, the expected civilian harm, the military advantage, the alternatives, the after-action review. “Child killers” names an enemy and pronounces him depraved. Aimed at Jews, it assigns collective guilt. A synagogue or a Hillel chapter, a Jewish student or an Israeli speaker or a Jewish-owned business can all be made to stand in for the Israeli state. The epithet follows Jews into schools and shops, onto streets and campuses, and up to the doors of communal buildings. When “child killer” is shouted at a synagogue or a Jewish student, it has left the realm of one strike or one minister and is an assignment of blood guilt.
