SETTLER-COLONIALISM

“Settler-colonialism” is an academic term for societies in which an incoming population builds lasting communities and seeks control over territory. In Patrick Wolfe’s influential formulation, settler colonialism is a continuing structure with territory as its central object. Lorenzo Veracini later distinguished it from ordinary colonial rule, in which the colonizer governs from abroad, extracts labor, or administers a dependency. The settler founds a new society in the place itself. Applied to Israel, the term is the scholarly form of the colonizer libel.

Standard settler-colonial models involve an imperial metropole, Britain in Australia, France in Algeria, Spain in the Americas. Zionism had no Jewish mother country. Jews did not arrive as agents of a Jewish empire, backed by a homeland capable of recalling them, funding them, or absorbing the fruits of conquest. They were a dispersed people, often persecuted, legally vulnerable, and stateless, and many came as refugees from European power. After the Holocaust, Jewish self-rule was a question of physical survival.

Jews were not foreign to the land. Jewish life there long predated modern Zionism, and Jewish religious, legal, liturgical, and historical memory remained tied to Jerusalem, Hebron, Safed, Tiberias, and the wider land of Israel across centuries of exile. Two peoples formed attachments to the same land under radically different historical conditions, and the framework cannot hold both.

A large share of Israeli Jews descend from Middle Eastern and North African communities. Many came from Iraq, Yemen, Egypt, Syria, Libya, Morocco, Tunisia, Algeria, and Iran under pressure, expulsion, dispossession, or fear. The antizionist version needs Israel to appear white, European, and alien, and Mizrahi history cuts through that image.

Britain issued the Balfour Declaration and governed Palestine under an imperial mandate. British policy repeatedly constrained Jewish immigration, most fatefully in the 1939 White Paper, which restricted Jewish entry into Palestine on the eve of Europe’s catastrophe. Zionist militias later fought British rule. The UN Partition Plan of 1947 proposed two states, one Jewish and one Arab. The Jewish leadership accepted the principle of partition, Arab leaders rejected it, and war followed. The war produced Palestinian flight and expulsion, and it produced Jewish survival against invading armies. The theory describes Palestinian loss and leaves out Jewish vulnerability, Arab rejection of partition, regional invasion, and the later expulsion or flight of Jews from Arab and Muslim-majority countries.

The West Bank is the strongest ground for the term’s users. Israeli communities beyond the 1949 armistice lines raise questions of property, military rule, legal inequality, annexationist ambition, and Palestinian self-determination. Post-1967 settlement policy is one subject and Israel’s existence another.

Fayez Sayegh’s Zionist Colonialism in Palestine, published by the PLO Research Center in 1965, described Zionism as a racially exclusive settler project. Maxime Rodinson’s Israel: A Colonial-Settler State? developed the argument for Marxist and postcolonial audiences after 1967. Edward Said moved the Palestinian case into Western academic language, where Zionism was described through the categories of European imperialism, Orientalism, and indigenous dispossession. Later settler-colonial studies supplied the vocabulary now common in classrooms, teach-ins, boycott campaigns, and protest statements.

In activist use the theory arranges the actors before the evidence is weighed, with Palestinians as indigenous victims, Jews as foreign settlers, Israel as the colonial structure, and decolonization as the remedy. A settlement can be dismantled, a border negotiated, military rule ended. If Israel itself is the settler-colonial object, compromise looks like collaboration and Jewish self-rule is marked for removal. Its usual companions are “decolonization,” “land back,” and “from the river to the sea.” Applied to Israel, decolonization means undoing the only Jewish state.

The term changes the moral status of violence. When Israelis are settlers and only settlers, attacks on them are redescribed as anti-colonial resistance, and civilian murder is a cost of liberation. The massacres of October 7 were explained in parts of antizionist discourse as the inevitable eruption of the colonized against the colonizer. A vocabulary that turns a whole people into a colonial category weakens the moral barrier against harming them.
