APARTHEID WALL

“Apartheid Wall” is the antizionist name for Israel’s West Bank security barrier, approved by the cabinet in June 2002 at the height of the Second Intifada. The barrier is a system of fences along most of the route and concrete slabs near cities, with patrol roads, ditches and monitored crossings. The idiom picks the most dramatic and exaggerated image and makes it stand for the whole. Attaching “apartheid” then turns a defensive project into a monument of racial supremacy.

The barrier exists to stop attackers from crossing out of the West Bank into Israeli cities. In a 2004 Washington Institute brief, IDF Col. Zohar Palti wrote that before the northern section was finished, 35 successful suicide attacks traced to Nablus and Jenin had killed 156 Israeli civilians. Once that section stood, attacks from the same area fell sharply. Intelligence work, arrests and raids contributed as well.

Campus demonstrations and NGO materials circulate the compound noun as though it were the structure’s ordinary name. Mock walls have gone up at universities across North America, Europe and Australia, usually during Israeli Apartheid Week. The displays present the barrier as South Africa’s heir and rarely mention the suicide bombings that preceded construction. The audience sees the concrete and never hears about the buses.

On July 9, 2004, the International Court of Justice issued an advisory opinion called Legal Consequences of the Construction of a Wall in the Occupied Palestinian Territory. The court found that building the barrier in occupied territory, including East Jerusalem, ran contrary to international law. It called on Israel to halt construction, dismantle the completed sections there, and compensate those harmed. Judge Thomas Buergenthal dissented on the ground that the court had reached an answer without the evidence required to give one. The opinion is now a stock citation in BDS materials, quoted as though it closed the case. An advisory opinion is advice requested by the UN, issued without the lawsuit in which two countries argue their full evidence. Israel declined to participate, arguing that the court never had the security facts needed to judge fairly.

In UN and NGO usage the phrase often appears without quotation marks, as plain description. Once the name is ordinary, the claim inside it is ordinary too, that Israel built the barrier to cage Palestinians by race. Holding that picture requires pushing the Second Intifada out of mind, though the cabinet approved construction in the middle of a bombing campaign against buses and cafés.

The words also inherit the South African ending. Apartheid was torn down. If Israel’s barrier is an apartheid wall, then not only should there be a demolition of this barrier, but there should also be an end to the state as well. A security measure is made proof that the Jewish state is criminal in form.

After Hamas seized Gaza in 2007 and hundreds of thousands of Palestinians breached the Gaza-Egypt border in January 2008, Egypt moved to fortify its own frontier. David Schenker reported that Egypt planned a Gaza border barrier with $23 million in American assistance and expected advice from the Army Corps of Engineers. Egyptian foreign minister Ahmed Aboul Gheit, who had earlier criticized Israel’s barrier, said, “Whoever wishes to build a security fence on his land is free to do that.” No slogan attached itself to Egypt’s project, and no mock Egyptian walls appeared on college quads.

The expression took hold during the first construction phases in 2002 and 2003, and the language was already waiting. The Israel-South Africa comparison had circulated through Soviet and Arab League diplomacy throughout the Cold War. UN General Assembly Resolution 3151G grouped Zionism with South African racism and Portuguese colonialism in 1973, and Resolution 3379 declared two years later that Zionism was “a form of racism and racial discrimination.” The General Assembly revoked that resolution in 1991. The 2001 Durban Conference renewed the comparison, with an NGO Forum declaration calling Israel a “racist, apartheid state” and demanding boycotts on the South African model. When the barrier rose a year later, activists had the phrase ready and a photograph to go with it. Activist usage then absorbed the 2004 ICJ opinion about the barrier’s legality into the much larger apartheid libel.

Saying “Apartheid Wall” announces acceptance of the South Africa comparison, while saying “security barrier” or “fence” risks being read as denial. The wording separates insiders from suspects. The name also moves the argument from policy to crime. If the barrier is an apartheid wall, those who build or fund or defend it are accomplices. The phrase therefore keeps company with demands for sanctions, boycotts and academic exclusion.

Israel’s own Supreme Court never treated the barrier as untouchable. In Beit Sourik Village Council v. Government of Israel, the Court accepted that security could justify construction and refused to let the route serve political annexation. It struck down sections where the harm to Palestinian villagers outweighed the security gained and ordered the path redrawn. A racist monolith is not redrawn by the country’s own judges. The barrier restricts movement and has cut communities off from farmland and clinics, and gates and permits govern daily life along it. A security measure in a violent conflict is not Afrikaner race law.
