GLOBALIZE THE INTIFADA

“Globalize the Intifada” urges the intifada to spread beyond Israel and the Palestinian territories. The Arabic word intifada means a shaking-off, an uprising. In Palestinian history it refers to two named campaigns, the First Intifada that began in 1987 and the Second Intifada that began in 2000. The First involved strikes, demonstrations, stone-throwing, riots, and some fatal attacks. The Second was far deadlier, with suicide bombings against buses and restaurants, city streets, hotels and clubs. The ADL summarizes that period as one in which Palestinian violence turned into terrorism aimed at Israeli civilians, leaving more than 1,000 Israelis dead and thousands severely wounded.

The chant spread after October 7, 2023 through anti-Israel demonstrations in the United States, Britain, Australia, and across Europe. It appeared at street protests and campus encampments, on placards and social-media graphics, in chants outside Jewish or Israel-linked sites. On December 5, 2023, Representative Elise Stefanik pressed Harvard President Claudine Gay at a congressional hearing about campus calls for “intifada” and “intifada revolution.” Gay termed the rhetoric personally abhorrent and declined to say categorically whether it broke Harvard’s bullying and harassment rules.

The American Jewish Committee’s State of Antisemitism in America 2025 Report found that 88 percent of U.S. Jews said seeing or hearing “Globalize the Intifada” would make them feel unsafe to some degree. The AJC describes the phrase as a call for aggressive resistance against Israel and its supporters, and notes that most American Jews read it as encouraging violence against Jews, Israelis, and those who back them.

In November 2024, supporters of the Israeli club Maccabi Tel Aviv were attacked in Amsterdam. Reuters reported that Mayor Femke Halsema called the attackers “antisemitic hit-and-run squads” while also noting anti-Arab conduct by some Maccabi fans before the match. On December 14, 2025, gunmen killed 15 people at a Hanukkah celebration at Bondi Beach in Sydney, opening fire with shotguns and a hunting rifle on hundreds of people, according to the Associated Press. New South Wales then moved to ban “globalise the intifada,” with the state government branding it hate speech and an encouragement of violence. In Britain, the Metropolitan Police and Greater Manchester Police announced on December 17, 2025, that targeted use of the phrase at future protests could prompt police action and arrests. The forces said the context had changed and that officers would use public-order powers where necessary, including around London synagogues during services.

The slogan extends the battlefield beyond Israel. Once the intifada is globalized, a Jewish student or a synagogue, a community center, an Israeli soccer fan or a company with Israeli ties is folded into the same enemy network. In November 2023, Within Our Lifetime briefly posted maps of New York Jewish organizations and said they had “blood on their hands.” The New York Jewish Week reported that the group calls for intifada and Israel’s destruction, and that the posts drew NYPD attention and public condemnation. The Boston Mapping Project had supplied a template in 2022, naming hundreds of organizations and closing, according to the ADL, with the line that every entity has an address and every network can be disrupted.

When Jews say the phrase sounds like a threat, they are told they misunderstand Arabic, or fear Palestinian freedom, or confuse criticism of Israel with antisemitism. That answer ignores the history attached to the word and the settings in which it is aimed at Jews.

Earlier protest language stayed on Palestine itself, with “Free Palestine,” “End the Occupation,” and “From the River to the Sea.” “Globalize the Intifada” asks for the export of the struggle. It sits inside an older antizionist habit of treating Jewish institutions abroad as proxies for Israel. Soviet propaganda portrayed Zionism as a worldwide system, and Arab nationalist and Islamist literature often cast it as a global enemy. The chant makes Jewish institutions and Israeli-linked events, Zionist speakers and synagogues, campus Hillels, businesses, and cultural groups into local fronts in a worldwide fight.

The slogan supplies deniability. Challenged, a speaker insists that intifada only means uprising or resistance or liberation. Shouted in a crowd after October 7, or displayed near a Jewish building, it sounds like a threat to Jews who remember what the Second Intifada did. Governments and police now treat the words as a safety question, and the decisions in New South Wales and Britain are contested on free-speech grounds. The phrase carries the memory of civilian targeting into Jewish life far from Israel and uses the language of resistance to license intimidation.
