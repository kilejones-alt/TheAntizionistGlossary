ZIO / ZIONAZI

“Zio” and “Zionazi” are slurs built from the word Zionist. “Zio” is a clipped, sneering shorthand that reduces a person to a single political identity. “Zionazi” welds the Jewish national movement to the government that murdered six million Jews. Zionism is a movement for Jewish self-determination, and Nazism was the machinery built to exterminate Jews. Calling a Zionist a Nazi recasts Jews, or anyone tied to Jewish national identity, as perpetrators of the very crime committed against them.

“Zio” was a favorite of the American white supremacist David Duke and circulated for years in far-right forums before drifting into left-wing activist spaces. By 2016 it had traveled far enough that Britain’s Labour Party commissioned an inquiry into antisemitism in its own ranks. The resulting Chakrabarti Report judged “Zio” a “modern-day racist epithet” and recommended that it have no place in party discourse. Jeremy Corbyn, then the party’s leader, pronounced it “a vile epithet.” The following year it surfaced among organizers of the Chicago Dyke March, who had expelled Jewish marchers carrying rainbow flags bearing the Star of David.

“Zio” lives mostly in digital exchanges, in posts, comments and short-form content on X, TikTok and Instagram. “Zionazi” and formulations like “Zionists = Nazis” appear on protest signs and graffiti, in projections and performance art. Nazi-comparison signs, banners and projections appeared during the spring 2024 campus encampments at Columbia, NYU and the University of Texas.

At Berlin’s Mercedes-Benz Arena in May 2023, the rock musician Roger Waters performed in a long black coat with a red armband, an outfit police said resembled an SS officer’s uniform, while firing a prop rifle toward the crowd. Berlin police opened a criminal investigation for suspected incitement, noting that the display could be read as glorifying Nazi rule. Waters described it as theater and a critique of fascism. The police forwarded the case to prosecutors.

In December 2025 at London’s Roundhouse, during an anniversary performance of the album XTRMNTR, Primal Scream played their song “Swastika Eyes” beneath a video that placed a swastika inside a Star of David over the eyes of Benjamin Netanyahu and other political figures. The footage mixed images of destruction in Gaza with the caption “Our government is complicit in genocide.” The Community Security Trust reported the band to the Metropolitan Police, and the venue apologized for imagery it deemed highly offensive. The band defended the video as “a piece of art” meant to provoke debate, then dropped the graphic from later shows.

Scholars call the move “Holocaust inversion.” Israelis or Zionists are the new Nazis, and their enemies the new victims. Jewish suffering is repurposed and handed back to Jews as an indictment. The inversion flattens history, since a checkpoint is not a cattle car and a war against a terror group is not the Final Solution. It also implies betrayal, that the lessons of the Holocaust have been reversed by the very people who survived it. The International Holocaust Remembrance Alliance’s working definition of antisemitism lists “drawing comparisons of contemporary Israeli policy to that of the Nazis” among its examples.

The Soviet Union systematized the equation after the 1967 Six-Day War, when Soviet presses poured out books, articles and cartoons binding Zionism to fascism and racism. Izabella Tabarovsky has documented that campaign’s scale and themes from the archives. The Romanian intelligence defector Ion Mihai Pacepa later described a coordinated KGB effort he called Operation SIG, designed to seed antizionist material across the Arab world, where earlier Nazi propaganda had already prepared the soil. Mahmoud Abbas, who would later lead the Palestinian Authority, wrote a 1982 dissertation in Moscow that advanced claims linking Zionism with Nazism. From there the language migrated into Western activist and academic circles, detached from its origins and keeping its wording. “Zio” is newer, a shorthand that spread through the online forums of the 2000s.

Together the words collapse a person’s identity into an indictment and move the fight from specific policies to total condemnation. Inside polarized movements, using them signals intensity and belonging. In institutional settings they ignite disputes over free speech and harassment. If Zionists are Nazis, their claims need no answer, because nobody negotiates with Nazis.
