ANTI-ZIONISM (1880–1947)

Antizionism is a form of Jew-hatred prevalent in institutions, activism, and the progressive left which use libels such as colonizer, genocide, and apartheid to target, marginalize, and harm Jews and end their connection to the land of Israel. Before 1948 the hyphenated word, anti-Zionism, named a different argument.

Early anti-Zionism was a coalition of religious, integrationist, socialist, and Arab political objections. Its participants disagreed about Jewish peoplehood and nationalism, class solidarity and immigration, and the political future of Palestine. Jewish opponents of Zionism feared the allegation of dual loyalty, rejected nationalism on religious grounds, preferred socialist universalism, or advocated a binational arrangement, and none of them treated Jewish collective existence as a contaminant. Beside them stood movements that rejected Jewish national rights altogether. Arab opposition grew from resistance to immigration, land purchase, British policy, and the prospect of a Jewish state. These disputes took place before a Jewish state existed, over a project whose outcome had not been settled. The Holocaust, the refugee crisis, and the 1947 partition debate changed the stakes of every earlier position.

Israel’s establishment in May 1948 gave antizionism a living country as its object. The question was now whether Jewish sovereignty that already existed should be accepted, contained, reversed, or destroyed. War, displacement, refugee claims, borders, and the territories captured in 1967 gave the argument new ground. Opposition was now a politics directed at a functioning country and at the legitimacy of its continued national existence. Here the Jewish anti-Zionisms of the pre-state years end and antizionism begins.

From 1948 through October 6, 2023, several political traditions gave antizionism its vocabulary. Arab nationalism, Soviet propaganda, Third World and Non-Aligned diplomacy, Western radical politics, postcolonial theory, NGO activism, and boycott campaigns circulated the libels that cast Zionism as racism, colonialism, imperialism, Nazism, or apartheid. The 1975 UN resolution equating Zionism with racism gave them an institutional landmark. The Durban NGO forum of 2001 and later BDS activism carried the indictment into human-rights organizations, universities, churches, unions, and professional associations. Criticism of particular Israeli governments increasingly shared its language with a campaign against Jewish statehood itself.

The Hamas-led attacks of October 7, 2023 activated that system at mass scale. In the weeks and months that followed, slogans, encampments, boycotts, professional campaigns, institutional statements, and confrontations with Jewish students gave the movement a visibility and social reach no earlier period had produced. Claims about genocide, apartheid, settler-colonialism, resistance, and decolonization moved rapidly between demonstrations, universities, cultural institutions, social media, and professional bodies. Exclusion, intimidation, collective blame, and demands for ideological conformity operate through language presented as ordinary politics.

Antizionism turns Israel, Zionism, and Zionists into a symbolic category of exceptional guilt. It treats the only Jewish-majority state as uniquely illegitimate, and the guilt migrates onto Jews and Jewish institutions elsewhere. Criticism of Israeli conduct is specific, rests on evidence, and holds Israel to the standards applied to other states and peoples. Antizionism licenses demonization, collective responsibility, exclusion, and the denial of Jewish political equality. Antizionism is a form of Jew-hatred which uses libels to demonize the state of Israel and mark Jews for violence.
