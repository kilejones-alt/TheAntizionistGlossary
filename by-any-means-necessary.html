BY ANY MEANS NECESSARY

“By any means necessary” is a permission slip. In ordinary speech it can signal determination or urgency, a refusal to wait or back down. Antizionists use this, especially after October 7, 2023, and place it in a category where problematic limitations or legal methods are void in the pursuit of Palestinian liberation. The wording rarely says “kill civilians” or “take hostages” or “fire rockets at towns.” It declines to draw the line. Applied to Palestinian armed action against Israel, that open boundary excuses attacks on civilians while keeping the speaker one polite step removed from the specifics.

Malcolm X gave the expression a durable place in American political memory. At the founding rally of the Organization of Afro-American Unity in New York on June 28, 1964, he said the OAAU sought freedom, justice, and equality “by any means necessary.” He was speaking about Black self-defense against racist violence and the failure of the state to protect Black life. The OAAU charter also invoked the UN Charter and the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, the U.S. Constitution and the Bill of Rights. His words assumed a victim defending himself.

The laws of war reject the notion that a just cause licenses any method. The principle of distinction requires fighters to separate civilians from combatants. Hostage-taking is forbidden, and deliberate attacks on civilians are war crimes. A movement may call itself liberation or resistance or self-defense, and none of those names erases the protection owed to civilians.

The phrase traveled after October 7 in company with “resistance in all its forms,” “From the River to the Sea,” and “Globalize the Intifada.” Together they translated Palestinian violence into a story of liberation and stepped around the specific acts committed against Israeli civilians. National Students for Justice in Palestine’s October 2023 Day of Resistance Toolkit called the Hamas-led attack a “historic win for the Palestinian resistance,” urged chapters to “normalize the resistance,” and said confrontation was required “by any means necessary.” It listed armed struggle among legitimate forms of resistance and asserted that settlers are not civilians in the sense of international law. That assertion is a direct assault on civilian status itself. Malcolm X’s words grew out of self-defense under racial terror, and the toolkit bolts them onto an armed assault that murdered civilians and took hostages.

On July 27, 2024, a rocket struck a football field in the Druze village of Majdal Shams and killed twelve children and teenagers. Israel and the United States blamed Hezbollah, and Hezbollah denied responsibility. The dead were not combatants by any definition. A slogan that blesses resistance before it identifies the target cannot condemn that strike.

The premise underneath the phrase is that the goal governs the act. Civilian protection, the prohibition on hostages and the whole body of the laws of war are secondary to liberation. A speaker need not say “I support the murder of civilians at a music festival.” The speaker says “by any means necessary” or “resistance in all its forms,” and the violence stays available for praise without fingerprints.

The lineage has three strands. The American force comes from Malcolm X and the OAAU. The second strand is anti-colonial theory. In The Wretched of the Earth Frantz Fanon gave anti-colonial violence a psychological and redemptive meaning and argued that it could help a colonized people recover agency, and Jean-Paul Sartre’s preface carried that argument into European intellectual life. Campus writing later absorbed a simplified version in which decolonization meant struggle, struggle meant resistance, and resistance escaped ordinary moral limits. Transferred to Israel, that thinking recasts killing as dignity or return or liberation even when the victims are civilians. The third strand is Palestinian militant doctrine. Hamas’s 2017 Document of General Principles and Policies calls resistance to occupation “with all means and methods” legitimate and gives armed resistance a central place, in the same text that refuses to recognize Israel and demands the full liberation of Palestine from the river to the sea.

The phrase pre-clears the violence. It never shows that a given attack was lawful and asks the audience to judge the act by its aim instead of its victim. A person who declines to bless “resistance in all its forms” is branded weak or colonial or indifferent to Palestinian suffering. Jewish students feel this pressure most sharply, because they are so often asked to denounce Israel while no one is asked to denounce Hamas. Raise Nova or Be’eri, the hostages or the sexual violence or the rocket fire, and the question itself is recast as bad faith. The slogan trains an audience to excuse the next attack before anyone knows what it will be.

Reasons do not legalize attacks on civilians or the taking of hostages, however urgent they sound. The legal questions remain, who was targeted, whether civilians were attacked directly, whether hostages were taken. “Any means” exists to keep those questions from being asked.
