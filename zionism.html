ZIONISM (1880–1947)

Zionism is the Jewish national movement for self-determination in the ancestral land of Israel. In its pre-state period, from the late nineteenth century to 1947, it argued that Jews were a people, with a shared history and language, a living memory and a homeland. The modern movement arose in Europe because emancipation had failed to keep Jews safe. Legal equality did not end exclusion, and assimilation did not end antisemitism. Leon Pinsker drew this conclusion in 1882, when his Auto-Emancipation argued that Jews could not wait for others to free them. A stateless people survives by permission, and permission can be withdrawn. Zionism’s answer to that danger was a state, a place where Jews became responsible for their own rescue. Zionism’s answer was Israel.

Pogroms swept the Russian Empire while restrictions persisted across the continent. The Dreyfus Affair in France showed that Jews could be citizens on paper and targets in practice. Theodor Herzl gave the crisis its most famous answer in The Jewish State in 1896, and a year later the First Zionist Congress in Basel turned his pamphlet into an organized international effort.

Before 1948, Zionism meant practical national construction. Its adherents revived Hebrew and bought land, founded farming settlements and built towns. They opened schools and newspapers, labor federations and health funds, and they organized defense groups and diplomacy. The movement built the organs of Jewish public life under Ottoman rule and then under the British Mandate.

The Basel Program of 1897 declared that Zionism sought a publicly and legally secured home in Palestine for the Jewish people. The early movement wanted international recognition as well as settlement, which is why Herzl and the World Zionist Organization courted empires and governments. That standing arrived on November 2, 1917, when the Balfour Declaration committed Britain to a national home for the Jewish people in Palestine. The same letter said nothing should prejudice the civil and religious rights of existing non-Jewish communities. The League of Nations later wrote the commitment into the Mandate for Palestine. Zionists read all of this as international recognition of Jewish national return. Arab leaders increasingly read it as a foreign-backed project that threatened Arab majority rule in the country. The same documents launched two opposite stories.

By the 1930s and 1940s the Yishuv, the Jewish community of Mandate Palestine, had representative bodies and Hebrew schools, labor institutions and farming settlements, welfare networks and armed defense groups. The Haganah was founded in 1920 and grew into the Yishuv’s main defense force, and the Palmach followed in 1941. The Irgun and Lehi broke away to the right and used harsher methods against both Arabs and the British, and the divisions among them were serious and sometimes bitter. Together these groups showed that Jewish dependence on outside protection was ending. When the United Nations voted for partition in November 1947, much of the state’s civic life already existed.

The deeper claim of pre-state Zionism was that Jewish safety could not rest on the kindness of host societies. The movement never said every Jew had to leave the diaspora. It said the Jewish people required a national center and a place of refuge, along with the capacity to defend Jewish life when other states failed or refused. The British White Paper of 1939 capped Jewish immigration at 75,000 over five years and made further entry dependent on Arab consent. It appeared just as European Jews faced Nazi persecution and, soon after, extermination. For Zionists the policy confirmed the core lesson. When Jews most needed entry, others could close the gate.

Zionism also insisted that Jews had never become strangers to their own past. Attachment to the land was carried through prayer and law, memory and pilgrimage, settlement and repeated return. Herzl’s pamphlet gave modern statecraft to an attachment older than most countries. The land was not empty. Arab communities lived there, and Arab opposition grew as Jewish immigration and land purchase increased. The Zionist answer was that Jewish self-rule remained necessary because Arab rejection was real and Jewish danger was real too.

Several streams fed the movement. The oldest was the Jewish attachment to the land of Israel. European nationalism was teaching many peoples to seek self-rule, and modern antisemitism showed Jews that integration alone could fail. Practical settlement had begun before Herzl, when Hovevei Zion and other early groups supported Jewish farming communities in Ottoman Palestine. Moses Hess had linked Jewish renewal with return two decades before Pinsker wrote. Ahad Ha’am pressed for Hebrew culture and inner renewal, while Herzl supplied diplomatic urgency and a mass organization.

The movement was plural from the start. Labor Zionists made settlement and Hebrew labor and collective institutions central to the project. Religious Zionists read return through Jewish tradition. Revisionists, following Ze’ev Jabotinsky, put greater stress on defense and Jewish power. These branches argued over religion and socialism, over diplomacy and force, over borders and the kind of society to build. They shared one premise, that Jews were a people entitled to national life in their historic homeland.

The international road ran through the Balfour Declaration and the Mandate, through British limits and reversals, through wartime catastrophe and postwar displaced-persons camps. It ended at the UN partition vote of November 1947. Resolution 181 recommended separate Jewish and Arab states with an economic union, plus a special international status for Jerusalem. The Jewish Agency cooperated with the plan despite real unhappiness over borders. Arab leaders rejected partition outright, and civil war followed. A people facing extermination and then homelessness needed entry and power, and a state able to say yes when others said no.

Pre-state Zionism organized immigration and built towns and farms. It trained defenders and raised money. Above all it persuaded Jews that they were not condemned to permanent dependence. Jews had already tried loyalty and learning, commerce and citizenship, reform and socialism and assimilation, and still had no final court of appeal when states turned against them. By 1947 the movement had assembled population and language, land and institutions, defense and diplomacy. The vote in New York recognized what already existed.

After May 1948 the work of Zionism was keeping the Jewish state alive. The question now was whether the country could survive war and migration, terrorism and diplomatic isolation, internal division and repeated attempts to make Jewish statehood temporary. Israel declared independence on May 14, 1948, and armies from neighboring Arab states entered the war. The new state survived, and the war also produced Palestinian displacement and exile. Zionism now named Jewish survival through statehood, while Palestinians came to remember the same war as the Nakba.

No single party or leader owned Zionism in this period. Labor Zionists built many of the early state institutions. Revisionists reached power through Menachem Begin and Likud in 1977, and religious Zionists grew more central after 1967, especially around settlement and land. Liberal Zionists stressed democracy and the courts, civil rights and compromise. Mizrahi, Ethiopian and Russian-speaking Jews changed what the state was, as did the secular, the traditional and the Orthodox. Their common conviction was that Jews needed power of their own because Jewish safety could not depend on permission from others.

Inside Israel, Zionism was practical statecraft. It meant immigration and Hebrew public life, military service and memorial days, elections and schools and courts. The Law of Return, enacted in 1950, turned the promise of refuge into a legal right to immigrate. Holocaust survivors arrived first. Jews fled or were driven from Arab and Muslim-majority countries. The Middle East after 1948 produced more than one refugee story. Ethiopian Jews came through dramatic rescue operations and later migration. More than one million immigrants from the former Soviet Union followed from the late 1980s onward.

Those waves made Zionism less European and less uniform, and far more Middle Eastern than many critics admit. A state described abroad as a white colonial implant was home to Jews from Iraq and Yemen, Morocco and Iran, Ethiopia and Russia and Argentina. The demographics demolish the story of Israel as a European settler project imposed on the region from outside, which may be why the story rarely mentions them.

War kept reshaping the word. The 1956 Suez Crisis, the 1967 Six-Day War and the 1973 Yom Kippur War reinforced the Israeli belief that defeat could be existential. The 1967 victory removed immediate threats and brought the West Bank and East Jerusalem, Gaza and Sinai and the Golan Heights under Israeli control. Millions of Palestinians now lived under Israeli rule, and territory and settlement were the central disputes of the next half century. Before 1967, Zionism meant state survival inside contested borders. After it, Zionism was judged increasingly through occupation and rule over Palestinians.

Diplomacy altered Zionism too. UN Security Council Resolution 242 tied withdrawal from territories captured in 1967 to the end of hostilities and the right of every state in the area to live in peace within secure and recognized boundaries. The slogan version usually keeps only the withdrawal half. Israel returned Sinai to Egypt under the 1979 peace treaty, dismantling its settlements there, and signed peace with Jordan in 1994. The Oslo process of 1993 brought mutual recognition. Israel recognized the PLO as the representative of the Palestinian people, and the PLO recognized Israel’s right to exist in peace and security. None of these agreements ended the conflict. They showed that Zionism after 1948 included treaty, withdrawal and compromise.

Begin’s rise in 1977 moved Revisionist Zionism from opposition into government, and Camp David followed. A right-wing Zionist leader returned territory for peace, settlements included. The precedent refutes the claim that Zionism requires permanent possession of every inch captured in 1967. The Oslo years then created a different hope, interim Palestinian self-government and negotiations toward final status. The Second Intifada ended it. Suicide bombings against buses and cafés shattered much of the Israeli public’s trust that withdrawal and negotiation would bring safety.

The 2005 disengagement from Gaza deepened the doubt. Israel dismantled every settlement there and withdrew its permanent ground presence. Hamas won Palestinian legislative elections in 2006, seized Gaza from Fatah in 2007, and built an armed enclave. Rocket fire and repeated wars followed. For many Israelis, Gaza was the warning that leaving territory did not necessarily end the threat. Together with Sinai, it stands against the claim that Zionism only expands and never yields.

The lesson of the whole period was that Jewish survival requires the capacity to defend Jewish life. That capacity now had an army and borders, courts and elections, embassies and intelligence services and a standing refuge for Jews in danger. Before the state, Jews had petitions and allies and luck. Power brought hard choices. Israel had to decide how to fight wars and govern captured territory, how to treat Arab citizens and answer terrorism, how to keep a Jewish majority without abandoning democratic life. Those decisions determined budgets and borders, checkpoints and settlements, prisons and funerals.

Antizionist usage during these decades worked to turn the word away from Jewish self-determination and toward permanent guilt. Soviet, Arab nationalist and Islamist writing fastened the libels of racism and colonialism, Nazism and apartheid onto Zionism. UN General Assembly Resolution 3379 gave the campaign its most famous sentence in 1975 by declaring Zionism a form of racism. Its repeal in 1991 removed the resolution and left the slogan. The state antizionists opposed refused to disappear. It won wars, made treaties and absorbed Jews from persecution zones, and it stood at the center of Jewish life worldwide. As Israel grew stronger, the language against it grew harsher, and the claim moved from opposing particular policies to denying the state’s right to exist.

Israel survived conventional wars and absorbed mass immigration. It built a high-capacity economy and signed peace treaties with two neighbors. It held competitive elections through severe conflict. Such a history cannot be reduced to slogans about domination. Jews were still not universally safe, and they now had a state able to defend and rescue and receive them, a safety less romantic than acceptance and more durable.

Zionism after October 7 means what it meant before October 7. The Jewish people have the right to self-determination in their ancestral homeland, and the State of Israel has the right to exist, defend its citizens, and receive Jews who need refuge. The massacre changed the cost of forgetting it. On October 7, 2023, Hamas and other armed Palestinians crossed from Gaza into southern Israel. They murdered civilians in homes and towns, at a music festival and on military posts, and dragged 251 people into Gaza as hostages. Israel’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs lists more than 800 civilians murdered that day. AJC reported that the return of Master Sgt. Ran Gvili’s remains in January 2026 completed the accounting for all 251 hostages.

For Zionists, the event renewed the movement’s oldest premise, that Jews cannot stake survival on outside sympathy. A Jewish state can fail and suffer surprise. It can make errors and face bitter division. It still gives Jews an army and borders, rescue capacity and diplomacy and a place of return. Statehood supplies what emancipation, tolerance, and foreign protection never reliably supplied. The attack happened inside the Jewish state, and critics ask what good independence is if Israel cannot prevent a massacre. A state was never a promise of perfect safety. It is the ability to fight back, recover the kidnapped, bury the dead under one’s own flag, and keep others from deciding whether Jewish life is worth defending.

In antizionist usage after October 7, the word often means the opposite. Zionism is made a name for evil, for racism and colonialism, genocide and apartheid, militarism and Jewish power. The word stops identifying support for Jewish self-determination and marks people instead, students and scholars, artists and donors, synagogues and businesses. Zionism is treated less as a belief to debate than as a stain to attach. National Students for Justice in Palestine’s October 2023 Day of Resistance Toolkit called the attack a “historic win for the Palestinian resistance” and urged students to normalize resistance. It was written immediately after the murder and kidnapping of civilians, with months of Israeli military action still ahead.

The climate around diaspora Jews changed as well. The ADL counted 9,354 antisemitic incidents in the United States in 2024, the highest annual total in its audit’s history, with 58 percent containing elements related to Israel or Zionism. AJC’s 2025 State of Antisemitism in America report found deep concern among American Jews after October 7. This is the atmosphere through which the word Zionist now travels.

The word also turned into a test of belonging. Jews who rejected Zionism were held up as acceptable Jews, while Jews who defended Israel’s existence were treated as suspect. In many progressive spaces, Zionism stopped being one position among many and marked the line dividing Jews who could stay from Jews who had to be corrected or shamed or shown the door. The claim underneath is that Jewish national self-defense is uniquely illegitimate. Other peoples may speak of liberation and return, of land and safety and self-rule. Jews are told the same words mean supremacy in their mouths.

A person can oppose a prime minister or a war aim or a settlement policy without opposing Zionism, and Israelis do it every day. The language of antizionism after October 7 routinely skips that difference. It treats the existence of the Jewish state as the original wrong and reads every later event through that conclusion. Hamas’s own documents show what is at stake. The 1988 Hamas Covenant spoke of a struggle against Jews and cited religious language about killing them. The 2017 document softened some wording and still rejected any alternative to the full liberation of Palestine “from the river to the sea.” That document is sometimes cited as moderation because it accepts a Palestinian state on the 1967 lines as a consensus formula, yet the same pages refuse recognition of Israel. The dispute reaches beyond a border adjustment. Applied politically, it rejects Jewish statehood in the land.

When activists call October 7 resistance, many Jews hear the rest of the sentence, a demand that the one Jewish state disappear. The issue is whether Jewish self-determination remains a legitimate fact. This usage has a history. Earlier entries in this glossary trace the supply chain through Soviet antizionist writing and Arab nationalist rejection, Islamist war and postcolonial scholarship, Durban and BDS and campus activism. By October 2023 the language was already in place, and the massacre activated it.

The older Zionist line runs the other way. Herzl argued that legal equality without power left Jews exposed, and the Basel Program sought a publicly and legally secured home. The Balfour Declaration and the Mandate, then the partition vote and independence, moved that claim from movement to state. After 1948, Israel absorbed Holocaust survivors and Jews pushed out of Arab and Muslim countries, then Soviet Jews and Ethiopian Jews and others facing danger. Zionism was a claim to land and an answer to Jewish vulnerability.

The antizionist response to October 7 tried to reverse that lesson. The massacre was presented as proof of Palestinian liberation or Israeli weakness, as settler collapse or the end of Zionism’s future. A world willing to explain away the slaughter of Jews is exactly the world Zionism was built to survive. For Jews and Israel’s supporters, the word now names the right to defend themselves collectively and mourn without apology, to demand the hostages back and to insist that Jewish safety cannot be outsourced. The taking of the hostages was a crime against named human beings.

Antizionism is a form of Jew-hatred which uses libels to demonize the state of Israel and mark Jews for violence. For antizionist movements, the word Zionist is a target. It lets speakers avoid saying Jews while still identifying many Jews as the problem. A synagogue can be called Zionist, and so can a Hillel or a Jewish student or a kosher restaurant. The label carries blame without requiring the speaker to use older anti-Jewish language. The IHRA working definition holds that denying the Jewish people their right to self-determination, including by calling Israel’s existence a racist endeavor, may be antisemitic depending on context. It also lists holding Jews collectively responsible for Israel’s actions and comparing Israeli policy to Nazi policy. Those examples describe the use of Zionist after October 7 as a public mark of guilt. When the word is used to sort Jews for exclusion, it has stopped naming a belief at all.

A person can be a Zionist and criticize Israeli governments, support Palestinian rights, and oppose annexation or extremism or failed policy. Zionism is the claim that Jews have a right to national self-defense and statehood. What a person cannot honestly do is define Zionism as Jewish evil and then claim to be having a normal policy dispute. Antizionism has one destination, the end of Israel as a Jewish state. October 7 forced the hidden question into the open, whether Jews remain a self-governing people in the land. The Jewish fear surrounding that question rests on murders and kidnappings, harassment and vandalism, and a documented rise in antisemitic incidents across several countries.
