NAZI ANALOGY (MODERN-DAY NAZIS / NAZI STATE)

The Nazi analogy reaches Israel in two forms. “Modern-day Nazis” brands people and is aimed at Zionists, Israelis, and Jews who defend Israel. “Nazi state” brands the country. The first makes individuals heirs of the perpetrators, and the second makes the state the successor of the Third Reich. Both claim that the person or the polity is Nazism in a new costume.

Nazism was a racial-totalitarian project built on dictatorship and conquest, on antisemitic law and the systematic murder of six million Jews. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum defines the Holocaust as the systematic, state-sponsored persecution and murder of six million European Jews by Nazi Germany, its allies, and collaborators. The Nuremberg Laws of 1935 stripped Jews of citizenship and criminalized marriage and sexual relations between Jews and Germans classed as non-Jewish under race law. To attach the word Nazi to Israelis or to Israel takes the central crime committed against Jews and turns it into a weapon against the Jewish state. The International Holocaust Remembrance Alliance lists “drawing comparisons of contemporary Israeli policy to that of the Nazis” among its examples that may be antisemitic depending on context.

Israel holds elections and seats opposition parties, runs courts and grants Arab citizens the vote, places non-Jewish judges on the bench and subjects its military conduct to legal review. The label is historically false whether it is aimed at a person or at the state. Its aim is reversal. The state created after the destruction of European Jewry is recast as the heir to the power that tried to annihilate the Jews, and the people who survived genocide are recast as its authors.

The label travels through chants and placards, cartoons and graffiti, online graphics and boycott materials. Israeli leaders are given Hitler mustaches, swastikas are drawn over Israeli flags, Gaza is compared to Auschwitz, and the Warsaw Ghetto is invoked as though Israeli military power and Nazi extermination policy belonged to one category. The European Institute for the Study of Contemporary Antisemitism’s 2009 report on the “Nazi card” defines the problem as the use of Nazi terms or symbols in reference to Jews, Israel, Zionism, or aspects of Jewish experience.

The comparison has moved into official speech. In December 2023, Reuters reported that Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdogan said Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu was “no different” from Hitler and likened Israel’s Gaza campaign to the Nazi treatment of Jews, and in May 2024 Reuters reported him demanding that Israeli leaders face a genocide trial. After October 7, 2023, the Hamas-led massacre was followed by a wave of claims that Israel had turned Nazi-like through its war in Gaza. The comparison pushed Hamas’s crimes out of view and made Jewish grief after the massacre look hypocritical or staged.

The claim turns the Holocaust into a mirror, in which the Jews who were the victims of extermination are said to be the exterminators. It tells Jews that the survival project built in the shadow of their near-destruction is the thing that murdered their families. From there it works as a permission system. If Israel is Nazi Germany, those who support its existence can be treated as fascist sympathizers, and Jewish students and synagogues, Hillels, artists, donors and community organizations can be pressured, excluded, or threatened under the banner of opposing Nazism. The libel follows Jews into classrooms and workplaces, arts spaces, unions and professional life.

The analogy destroys distinctions. It blurs war and extermination, battlefield harm and death camps, emergency rule and racial dictatorship, civilian casualties and planned industrial murder. Once it is accepted, asking about Hamas tunnels or hostage-taking, rocket fire, human shields or proportionality is dismissed as evasion.

The Israel-Nazi comparison was central to Cold War antizionist language after Israel’s 1967 victory over Soviet-backed Arab states, when Soviet propaganda set out to discredit Zionism among postcolonial and Western-left audiences by equating it with racism and fascism, imperialism and Nazism. Yuri Ivanov’s Caution: Zionism!, published in English by Progress Publishers in 1970, cast Zionism as a sinister global force tied to fascism and conspiracy. UN General Assembly Resolution 3379 declared in 1975 that Zionism was “a form of racism and racial discrimination,” and it belonged to the same campaign. The resolution was revoked in 1991, and the vocabulary outlived it. A second channel ran through Arab public writing, including a 1984 Amman book by Mahmoud Abbas, later president of the Palestinian Authority, usually rendered as The Other Side: The Secret Relationship Between Nazism and Zionism. It was part of a wider effort to bind Zionism to Nazism and shift a share of the Holocaust’s guilt onto Zionists. Izabella Tabarovsky has traced how Soviet antizionism borrowed from tsarist and Nazi antisemitic material, refitted it in Marxist-Leninist language, and exported it through state publishing and international networks.

The analogy is a disqualification device. Israel no longer has to be engaged as a real country with real enemies, because it has been filed under ultimate evil. Negotiation sounds indecent, security claims sound fake, and Jewish fear sounds like manipulation. Cooperation with Israelis is relabeled as normalization with fascism, and Israeli scholars, artists and athletes are made untouchable. The analogy also degrades the memory it borrows from. Auschwitz is made a metaphor and the Warsaw Ghetto a protest prop, while the machinery of Nazi rule, its racial law, one-party dictatorship, ghettos, Einsatzgruppen, deportations, camps, and gas chambers, disappears. It tells Jews that the memory of the Holocaust no longer shields them from eliminationist language and can be turned against them.
