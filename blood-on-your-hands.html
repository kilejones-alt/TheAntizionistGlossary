BLOOD ON YOUR HANDS

“Blood on your hands” is a chant and a piece of theater. Protesters paint their palms red and raise them toward the cameras. The same red hand appears on pins and posters, in graffiti and online graphics. The image needs no caption, because red hands mean blood and blood means guilt.

In ordinary political life the phrase has been hurled at governments and generals, at corporations and presidents. In antizionist settings the target widens. Israel comes first, then Zionists, then Jewish organizations, students and congregations with no role in Israeli decision-making. A protest outside a ministry or an embassy addresses a government. The same gesture shouted at Jewish students, painted on Jewish homes or displayed outside a synagogue informs Jews that they carry blood guilt because of who they are presumed to be.

The gesture surged after the Hamas attacks of October 7, 2023 and the war that followed. Red hands appeared at street protests and campus actions, in graffiti and celebrity activism. In March 2024, red-hand pins appeared at the Academy Awards through the Artists4Ceasefire campaign, which described the pin as a call for a ceasefire and humanitarian aid, along with the release of the hostages. Many Jews could not watch a red hand raised during a war against Israel and see anything but the photograph from Ramallah.

In June 2024, activists hurled red paint at the homes of Brooklyn Museum leaders, among them the museum’s director Anne Pasternak, alongside the words “Blood on your hands” and “White Supremacist Zionist.” The month before, vandals defaced the Wall of the Righteous at the Paris Holocaust memorial with blood-red hands. That wall honors French citizens who rescued Jews during the Second World War. French officials later investigated the act as possible foreign interference meant to inflame antisemitic tension. In both cases the red hand was applied directly to Jewish targets.

The ADL counted 9,354 antisemitic incidents in the United States in 2024, the highest annual total in the history of its audit, and 58 percent of them contained elements related to Israel or Zionism. The Community Security Trust recorded 3,528 antisemitic incidents in the United Kingdom that year, its second-highest total. This is the atmosphere in which “blood on your hands” traveled from protest language into pressure on Jewish communities.

The libel treats guilt as already decided. It asks neither whether a given strike was lawful nor whether the harm could have been avoided. Questions about Hamas fighting from civilian areas or hostages being nearby disappear. The demand underneath the gesture is confession. The target is expected to admit guilt and denounce Israel, or else wear the stain.

For Jews and Israelis the image cannot be separated from one day. On October 12, 2000, reservists Yosef Avrahami and Vadim Nurzhitz took a wrong turn into Ramallah and were brought to a Palestinian police station. A mob stormed the building, beat and stabbed the two men to death, and mutilated their bodies. One of the killers, Aziz Salha, raised his crimson palms at the station window, and the photograph was an emblem of the era. Israel arrested Salha in 2001 and a court sentenced him to life in prison. He walked free in the 2011 Gilad Shalit prisoner exchange and was deported to Gaza. In October 2024, Reuters reported that Hamas media and medics said he had been killed in an Israeli airstrike in Deir al-Balah.

The older lineage is the blood libel. For centuries Jews were accused of using non-Jewish blood for ritual purposes, an invention that fueled trials, expulsions and pogroms. Magda Teter’s Blood Libel: On the Trail of an Antisemitic Myth traces that myth’s endurance across European history. The pattern holds in the red hand. Bloodshed is attributed to Jews as a group, and the group is told to answer for it.

Civilian casualties and military law, Hamas tactics and hostage rescue, aid access and ceasefire politics all shrink into a single red print. Within the movement the hands also mark loyalty. Displaying them announces acceptance of the case against Israel, and declining to display them can be read as indifference. A synagogue, a Hillel or a Jewish community center is not the Israeli government. Most diaspora Jews command no soldiers and write no Israeli laws. Red hands at those doorsteps hold them liable anyway, and the display is an assignment of blood guilt.
