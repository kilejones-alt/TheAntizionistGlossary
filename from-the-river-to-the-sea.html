FROM THE RIVER TO THE SEA

“From the river to the sea” names the land between the Jordan River and the Mediterranean, the whole space that today holds Israel, the West Bank, and Gaza. In antizionist use it arrives in full, “From the river to the sea, Palestine will be free.” Israel sits inside the named space, and a single free Palestine across all of it leaves no room for a Jewish state. Without a limiting clause the chant reaches Tel Aviv and Haifa as surely as Hebron and Gaza City. Defenders say it calls for equal rights, democratic coexistence, or an end to Israeli military rule.

The territorial idea is old. The 1964 Palestinian National Charter described Palestine, within its British Mandate boundaries, as an indivisible territorial unit, and that language predates Israeli control of the West Bank and Gaza. The rhymed English chant has a shorter paper trail. The earliest clear English appearance located so far comes from the writer Fawaz Turki, whose 1980 Journal of Palestine Studies account of the 1979 Palestine Congress of North America gives the line, “The people will struggle till the total liberation of Palestine from the river to the sea.”

Hamas put the claim in religious terms in its 1988 covenant, which speaks of raising the banner of Allah over every inch of Palestine. Its 2017 document defines Palestine as running from the Jordan River in the east to the Mediterranean in the west, rejects any alternative to full liberation, and states that the establishment of Israel created no rights for what it calls the Zionist entity. The same text mentions a Palestinian state on the 1967 lines as a formula of national consensus and offers it without recognizing Israel. The documents claim the whole map, and the chant gives that claim a softer public sound.

After October 7, 2023, the slogan was among the most visible phrases in anti-Israel protest, at street marches and campus encampments, in online campaigns and in fights over the meaning of antisemitism. In November 2023 the House of Representatives censured Representative Rashida Tlaib in part over her use and defense of the phrase. On April 16, 2024 the House passed H. Res. 883, which called the slogan an antisemitic call for the eradication of Israel. The members who voted no, mostly progressive Democrats, argued that the measure conflated criticism of Israel with antisemitism.

The American Jewish Committee’s 2024 survey found that 64 percent of American Jews considered the statement antisemitic while 35 percent did not, and the Anti-Defamation League describes it as a call for a state stretching from the river to the sea that would dismantle the Jewish one. Meta’s Oversight Board ruled in September 2024 that three Facebook posts using the phrase broke no rules, finding they expressed solidarity with Palestinians without calling for violence or exclusion or praising Hamas. That ruling settled a content-moderation question.

By 2026 the phrase had entered statute. Queensland, Australia, passed the Fighting Antisemitism and Keeping Guns out of the Hands of Terrorists and Criminals Amendment Act 2026, which wrote a prohibited-expression offence into the state criminal code. The law names both “from the river to the sea” and “globalise the intifada” as prohibited expressions when they are publicly recited, distributed, or displayed in a way reasonably expected to make a member of the public feel menaced, harassed, or offended, subject to listed reasonable-excuse provisions.

The slogan erases the territorial distinction between Israel inside its pre-1967 armistice boundaries and the territories captured in 1967. The demand no longer stops at ending military rule in the West Bank or changing Gaza policy and reaches the entire state. A speaker may intend a single democratic state with equal rights for all, and even that version asks Jews to accept that their collective self-rule is uniquely illegitimate in the one place it was restored after exile, persecution and genocide. The speaker need not say “end Israel,” because the map says it for him. In protest spaces the line is also a loyalty test, and refusing to chant it can mark a person as suspect. The gentlest interpretation and the harshest arrive at the same place on the map.
